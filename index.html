About

About Pay to Play Politics

Independent coverage of the business of sports and the politics behind it.

Pay to Play Politics covers the intersection of sports business and public power: franchise valuations, stadium deals and public funding, labor, betting regulation, and media rights. The Wire gathers the day’s reporting from Sportico, Front Office Sports, Sports Business Journal, ESPN, Bloomberg, The Athletic, and statehouse reporting nationwide, and every headline links to the original publisher.

The Franchise Ledgers track all 183 teams across the NBA, NFL, MLB, MLS, NHL, WNBA, and NWSL: the controlling owner of each franchise, the year purchased and the price paid, today’s estimated value, and the stadium — what it cost and how it was financed. Values are published estimates from Forbes and Sportico; purchase prices are the reported price of the controlling stake.

The Owners & Politics section tracks what team owners give in federal elections, the Stadium Subsidy Tracker follows the public money in stadium deals, and Media Rights Deals lists the leagues’ national TV and streaming contracts. The Ownership Pipeline tracks who owns the teams — and how owners get there, from limited partner to controlling owner.

The site is published by Chmari L. Anderson. The wire refreshes every morning.

The Pipeline

Ownership Pipeline

Who owns the teams — and how someone becomes an owner. The controlling owners of all 185 franchises, the limited partners waiting in the wings, the rules that decide who can buy in, and the price of entry.

Zero of 124, then one. Before August 2026 there were zero Black majority or controlling owners across the 124 big-four franchises (NBA 30, NFL 32, MLB 30, NHL 32). The only two Black majority owners in modern big-four history were both NBA, both Charlotte: Robert L. Johnson, who won the Charlotte expansion franchise in December 2002 for a $300 million fee, and Michael Jordan, who bought control in 2010 for $275 million and sold his majority stake in 2023. The August 2026 Padres sale made Kwanza Jones the first Black woman to hold a majority/co-majority MLB stake — jointly with her husband José E. Feliciano, who is the league’s designated control person, on a reported ~40–45% stake. Whether the Padres count as “Black-controlled” depends on the definition used; see the Padres box in the MLB section.

Women control 27 teams. Across the seven leagues, 27 teams have a woman as principal or co-controlling owner, verified as of November 2026 (29 counting Seattle’s Samantha Holloway and Anaheim’s Susan Samueli as co-owners; a 30th is claimed — Boston Legacy FC — but could not be confirmed, so it is not counted). Several women control two teams: Gayle Benson (Saints and Pelicans), Ann Walton Kroenke (Nuggets and Avalanche), Dee Haslam (Browns and Crew), Clara Wu Tsai (Nets and Liberty), Gail Miller (Real Salt Lake and Utah Royals), Marian Ilitch (Tigers and Red Wings).

The path exists — and no Black owner has walked it yet. Seven minority-to-controlling-owner cases are verified across the leagues: Patrick Dumont (Mavericks), Gabe Plotkin and Rick Schnall (Hornets), Vivek Ranadivé (Kings), David Tepper (Panthers), Jimmy Haslam (Browns), and Michele Kang (Spirit). All seven are non-Black owners. No verified Black owner has completed the step from limited partner to controlling owner of a team.

How to read this page: controlling owners are identified from team and league announcements and public reporting as of November 2026; “controlling owner” means the person named as the team’s principal owner, governor, or control person. Race and gender are shown only where a reliable published source states them or the person self-identifies publicly. Limited-partner stakes are usually not disclosed, so most minority stakes are listed without a percentage — where a figure appears, it is the most commonly reported one and the source is named. Figures are not comparable across leagues: an expansion fee buys league entry, a sale price values an operating franchise, and a “valuation” in a controlling-stake deal is not the same as cash paid for 100%. Do not rank owners or leagues by these figures. Ownership changes fast — this is a snapshot as of November 27, 2026, and unclosed deals are marked as not closed, not presented as done.

Ownership rules (NBA): private equity was admitted in 2020 and expanded in November 2022 to endowments, pension funds and sovereign wealth funds. A single fund may hold up to 20% of a franchise (no more than the controlling owner’s stake); all institutional investors combined are capped at 30%. Funds must hold at least $750 million in assets, put no more than 25% of a fund’s assets in one team, and hold for at least five years. The teams-per-fund limit was five through late 2025, then raised to eight, effective December 3, 2025. Sovereign wealth funds are permitted as passive minority investors; the league granted an exception for the Qatar Investment Authority’s stake in Monumental Sports & Entertainment. A controlling owner must hold at least 15%. Older reporting puts the group-size limit at 25 investors (each minority partner at least 1%) and the debt limit at $475 million per team as of 2025 — neither is verified against a league publication. No published net-worth minimum for a controlling owner was found. There was no distinct “2024 private equity rules” change; the accurate framing is admitted 2020, expanded November 2022, teams-per-fund raised in December 2025.

Ownership rules (NFL): Resolution JC-7, adopted August 27, 2024 by a 31–1 vote (the Bengals the lone dissenter), lets approved private-equity funds own up to 10% of a team in total, with a minimum 3% stake per fund, a maximum of six teams per fund, and a six-year minimum hold; stakes are passive and non-voting, with no governance rights. Funds must hold at least $2 billion in committed capital, put no more than 20% of a fund’s capital in one team, and no individual limited partner may hold above 7.5% of a fund (3% for a current NFL owner or immediate family member). Approved funds: Arctos Partners, Ares Management, Sixth Street, and a consortium of Blackstone, Carlyle, CVC, Dynasty Equity and Ludis. A controlling owner must hold at least 30% of the franchise; ownership groups are capped at 25 owners. On May 22, 2024 owners voted to let new buyers borrow up to $1.4 billion in total ($700M operating debt + $700M acquisition debt), up from $1.2 billion. A May 2025 proposal to raise that to $1.5 billion was not verified as passed — $1.5 billion is not the published limit. No published net-worth minimum for a controlling owner was found.

Ownership rules (MLS): MLS is a single-entity league: an investor buys an equity stake in the league and the right to operate a club as an “operator-investor,” rather than buying a standalone club. Each ownership group must have a principal owner holding at least 35% of the franchise’s equity with sole authority to bind it, per Sheppard Mullin (2023) and Legal 500 — but U.S. Soccer updated its Professional League Standards in March 2026, lowering the minimum required principal-owner stake from 35% to 15%. That is a federation sanctioning standard, not necessarily a change to MLS’s internal rule; whether the 35% rule still applies was not verified. Private equity was admitted in 2020: funds may take stakes above $20 million and below 20% of a club; no club may sell more than 30% to institutional investors in total; a fund is limited to four teams. Qualified funds need at least $500 million in committed capital, and no single investor or group may hold more than 25% of the fund, invest more than 10% of the fund’s capital in one franchise, or concentrate more than 25% of the fund’s capital in MLS clubs. Investments are passive with limited governance rights. MLS does not allow investment from governmental or quasi-governmental authorities, nonprofits or charitable organisations, or investors that do not satisfy its minimum ownership criteria. The federation’s Division I standard requires a club’s principal owner plus ownership group to hold combined net worth of at least $70 million, excluding the team stake and primary residence (secondary summary; not verified against the USSF document). No MLS debt limit was found in a reliable source.

Ownership rules (NWSL): the NWSL is the most permissive league on control: unlike the others, it allows a private equity fund to own a majority stake in a team — Sixth Street Partners owns a majority stake in Bay FC. Multi-team private-equity rules, codified February–March 2024: a fund with investments in multiple teams is limited to minority stakes in no more than three NWSL teams at a time; a single fund’s minority investment in any team must be between 5% and 20% of that team’s equity; no more than 30% of a team’s total equity may be held by minority private-equity investors. Investing funds must have capital commitments of at least $100 million and hold the investment for at least five years; all transactions are subject to league approval and must give the club’s controlling owner a right of first purchase. The 2024 rules apply only to private equity funds; the same passive minority rights do not extend to sovereign wealth funds and pension funds, and the NWSL does not allow investments from governmental or quasi-governmental entities.
